About this book

A hobby, not a syllabus

This handbook keeps one promise: within a few minutes of every sitting, you will be making a sound worth listening to. It is written for someone who has never touched a synthesiser, but who knows what a track is in GarageBand and can count a bar of four.

SLOOP is a free, open‑source firmware by isod89 that turns the inexpensive M‑VAVE FM‑1 into a four‑track groovebox: three synthesisers and a drum machine, played live with your hands. This book is a gentle course through it, aimed squarely at atmospheric, unhurried electronic music. Drums, basslines and melodies arrive only once you have somewhere beautiful to put them.

Beta firmware, honestly labelled

This book is pinned to SLOOP 2.8.4 (30 October 2026), which its author still calls a beta: install at your own risk and expect the occasional rough edge. Maintenance releases follow quickly: 2.8.5 (4 November 2026) exists only to accept the different flash chip inside recently sold FM‑1s, so before installing, check the project's releases page for anything newer and read its notes; the lessons here describe 2.8.4 and stay valid until the guide itself changes. The documented safety nets are good ones, a rescue mode, a path back to the official firmware, and one‑file backups, and Chapter 2 teaches all three before anything else, so that the ordinary mishaps stay ordinary.

How instructions are written

Every instruction in this book uses the same five patterns, borrowed from SLOOP's own guide:

WrittenMeans
tap FXpress FX and let go without touching anything else (shorter than about half a second)
hold FXkeep FX pressed down while you do the next thing
FX+key 3hold FX, and while it is held, press white key 3
SEL+KNOB 2hold SEL, and while it is held, turn KNOB 2
key 1 … key 16the 16 white keys, counted from the lowest (key 1, the note F3) to the highest (key 16, G5)

One more convention from SLOOP itself: a value marked GO (LOAD, SAVE, NEW…) is deliberately hard to trigger by accident. Turn its knob one click and the screen asks AGAIN: …; turn one more click within about 1.5 seconds to actually do it.

GarageBand translation

Boxes like this one appear throughout. They translate what the FM‑1 is doing into the GarageBand ideas you already have, so nothing feels foreign for long.

How to use the lessons

1Chapter one

What SLOOP turns your FM‑1 into

Out of the box, the FM‑1 is a little FM synthesiser. With SLOOP installed, it becomes something friendlier and far more complete: a pocket studio with four tracks that loop together, played entirely by hand.

GarageBand translation

A track in SLOOP is a close cousin of a GarageBand track: an independent layer of music with its own instrument, its own loop and its own volume. Two honest differences: here you play each layer on the keys and SLOOP catches it into a loop that repeats, rather than dragging regions with a mouse; and each track is one live instrument with one sound at a time, not a lane that can hold many different regions. You still build the song the same way, by stacking layers.

The three synth tracks share eight voices between them (a voice is one note sounding at once); the drum track has six of its own. You never manage this: SLOOP does. One more ground rule worth knowing from day one: each synth track plays one sound at a time, and changing a track’s sound with PRESETS re‑voices everything already recorded on that track. Lesson 7 shows how to arrange around this.

Three ideas carry the whole instrument

1 · One knob picks the track. The ALGORITHM knob selects which track you are playing, recording and editing, on every screen (with the documented exceptions: it waits while a layer button is held, during a free take, on the SONG screen and in the menu). The screen takes on the colour of the selected track, so you always know where you are.

2 · Every function button has two lives. Tap a button and its settings pages open. Hold it and the 16 white keys and four knobs temporarily do something else; this is called a layer. Let go, and you are back to playing notes. The whole instrument is taps and holds; there is nothing deeper to memorise.

3 · The screen speaks two colours. White always means the thing you are touching right now. Red always means recording. Everything else is decoration.

Why this suits atmospheric music

SLOOP ships with granular clouds, drones, bowed metal and choir pads among its 153 sounds, a built‑in bed of rain, wind or vinyl crackle to sit underneath everything, and loops that can stretch to two bars per step for music that moves slowly. It has no factory patterns and no demo songs: every sound you hear will be yours.

SLOOP is built on Felucca by Leo Kuroshita (Hügelton Instruments) and is free software under the GPL‑3.0 licence. M‑VAVE and FM‑1 are trademarks of their owners; SLOOP is not affiliated with them.

2Chapter two

Install, back up, stay safe

Ten minutes of setup buys you real peace of mind: SLOOP installed, your work protected by backups, and a documented road back to the official firmware whenever you want it.

Installing SLOOP

  1. Open the installer page at isod89.github.io/sloop-fm1 in Chrome or Edge (Safari and Firefox will not work; the installer talks to the FM‑1 over a browser feature called Web MIDI that only Chrome‑family browsers provide).

  2. Connect the FM‑1 by USB with a data cable, plugged straight into the computer, no hub. Many USB‑C cables are charge‑only; if the installer cannot find the device, suspect the cable first. Switch the FM‑1 on.

  3. Press INSTALL on the page and allow MIDI access when the browser asks. Then wait, cable untouched, until the page says Done.

  4. The FM‑1 restarts on the SLOOP logo. That is the whole installation.

Updating later is just as easy

Future SLOOP versions install the same way, and an update keeps your projects, presets, samples, kits and settings. The installer checks every package twice before a single byte is written, and refuses a damaged one; the practical risk sits in interruptions, which is why the one rule below exists.

GarageBand translation

Think of firmware as swapping which app the hardware runs. Installing SLOOP does not "jailbreak" or modify the FM‑1 permanently; it simply loads a different program, and the official program can be loaded back the same way.

The one rule

Never unplug or switch off the FM‑1 while an install is running. If one does get interrupted, the documented behaviour is forgiving: the FM‑1 waits in update mode and pressing INSTALL again finishes the job, and USB rescue (next page) covers the stubborn cases. Treat careful cables and patience as the real protection.

Backing up: one file holds everything

SLOOP comes with a companion web editor that runs in the same browser (Chapter 14 tours it properly). For now you only need its single most important button.

  1. Open the editor at isod89.github.io/sloop-fm1/webapp/editor in Chrome or Edge with the FM‑1 on USB, press Connect, and allow MIDI.

  2. Go to the Song & sections page, find Backup, and press Save a backup. One file lands in your Downloads folder, named like SLOOP‑backup‑DATE.json.

When to back up

Before every firmware update, before going back to the official firmware, and whenever you finish something you would be sad to lose. Backups are also how you keep more than one song; Lesson 10 returns to this.

Autosave: the FM‑1 remembers by itself

Day to day you do not press save at all. Whenever playback is stopped and you have left the controls alone for about 2.5 seconds (at most once every 20 seconds), SLOOP quietly writes your working music to the device's memory. So before switching off: stop playback, take your hands off the controls for a few seconds, then power down; done that way, in the documented behaviour, it comes back on exactly as you left it. Switch off while playing, or the instant you stop, and the latest changes may not have been written yet. Autosave protects against forgetting, not against hardware failure or a beta bug: the backups above remain the real safety net for anything you love.

If things go wrong: the complete recovery kit

Read this page once now, so that if a bad day ever comes, you already know the documented way out of it. With a current backup in hand, nothing on this list needs to cost you music.

SLOOP will not start

Hold OCT− alone while switching the FM‑1 on. The screen shows SLOOP USB RESCUE; now install again from the browser installer as normal. (Fixed and made reliable in 2.8.)

An install stopped half‑way

The FM‑1 waits patiently in update mode. Press INSTALL on the installer page again.

A button or knob does the wrong thing

Hold OCT−+OCT+ together while switching on (or use the menu: hold HOME → SYSTEM → HARDWARE CALIBRATION) and follow the on‑screen prompts, pressing each control as asked.

Going back to the official firmware

  1. Save a backup first (previous page). Going back does not erase your SLOOP work, but the backup makes it certain.

  2. Use M‑VAVE's own updater, M‑UPGRADE: on m‑vave.com go to Downloads → PC Software → M‑UPGRADE, and separately fetch the official FM‑1 V16 firmware file (FM‑1.fwsc) from PC Firmware → FM‑1. Close every other app that uses MIDI, then flash the file with M‑UPGRADE.

  3. If M‑UPGRADE cannot find the FM‑1 (with SLOOP installed the device announces itself under SLOOP's own name, and on some systems M‑UPGRADE then does not list it): open the Clipper installer page at isod89.github.io/clipper‑fm1/webapp/installer and use its Return to the official firmware option with the same official file. This works with SLOOP still on the FM‑1; you do not need to install Clipper itself, and the file is checked before anything is written.

  4. To come back later, install SLOOP again and Restore your backup, which brings your sections, presets, samples and settings back from the file.

Odd settings after going back?

If the official firmware comes up with strange settings or a scrambled display, it is only reading the settings SLOOP left in memory; nothing is damaged. A factory reset in the official firmware (or M‑UPGRADE's full restore) clears it.

Why SLOOP's own guide no longer offers a browser "return to stock"

Up to version 2.7 the SLOOP installer had its own return‑to‑stock button. It was removed in 2.8 because it was not reliable on every system; the M‑UPGRADE route above, with the Clipper page as fallback, is the documented path for 2.8.4.

Meet the hardware

Every control on the FM‑1 at its real proportions, and the key map every lesson uses.

Traced from a photograph of the FM‑1. The OP1 … POLY legends belong to the stock firmware; under SLOOP, use the blue names and numbers. The dots mark gaps with no upper key. Round the back: stereo out, TRS MIDI in, USB‑C and the power switch.

Tap, hold, and the seven layers

Every function button leads two lives. Tap it and its settings pages open; tap again for the next page, or walk back and forth with SELECT. Hold it and a layer opens: while the button is down, the 16 white keys and KNOB 1–4 take on new jobs, drawn on the screen as sixteen tiles and four dials. Let go and you are instantly back to playing. The lessons introduce each layer as it becomes useful:

EDITerasers, one per sound or note3
ARPnote repeat (rolls)6
SEQthe 16 steps of the pattern8

ENV and LFO have no layer; tapping them opens their pages only. While any layer is held, keys 1, 5, 9 and 13 glow dimly: the first key of each row of tiles, landmarks for your fingers. PLAY always works, and SELECT is always the tempo.

Lock a layer open

Hold a layer's button and tap HOME: the layer stays open after you let go (LOCK on screen, the button blinks), leaving both hands free. Press any other button to release it.

GarageBand translation

TRACKS is your arrange window: every layer at a glance, with the playhead sweeping each loop. The dials under it are the four knobs, exactly as a channel strip's controls.

The screen's two colours, one more time

White is always the thing you are touching: the value a knob is changing, the key you are holding, the cursor. Red is always recording: the REC light, the red rec badge on a track, the red armed screen. If something is white, you moved it; if something is red, you are on tape. Everything in the lessons ahead reads through those two rules.

L1Lesson one · Atmosphere

Your first sound

No recording, no theory, no tempo. This lesson is simply you, one beautiful sound, and a room slowly filling with it.

starting from here
  • FM‑1 on, headphones or a speaker in the 3.5 mm output, MASTER low to start, then up to taste.
  • Tap HOME so you are on the TRACKS screen.
  • Turn ALGORITHM to track 1. The screen turns blue.
  • Want a totally clean slate? Tap ENV, then tap SAVE (its pages open), then turn SELECT right until the TOOLS page shows. Turn KNOB 4 (NEW) one click; the screen asks AGAIN: NEW; one more click does it. A new loop starts at 90 BPM with fresh sounds on all four tracks. Your saved song sections A–D are kept, but the working loop itself is replaced and only its last‑edited track can be undone, so once Lesson 10 has taught you sections, save a loop you like into one before reaching for NEW.

  1. Play a few white keys. Track 1 wakes up on a bass sound called 808 BOOM, one note at a time. Fine, but not why we are here.

  2. Turn PRESETS slowly to the right. You are browsing 153 sounds, grouped by kind, each labelled on screen (bass, keys, organ, pad, lead…). Keep going until the pads arrive, and stop on WARM PAD. Hold three or four white keys together: your first chord of the course.

  3. Find your register. Tap OCT− once or twice to move the whole keyboard down into darker territory; OCT+ brings it back up. Press both together to return to centre. The OCT lights glow whenever you are away from centre.

  4. Add space. Tap FX and check the title says FX (its button remembers its last page; tap again or turn SELECT left until it does). Four dials show. KNOB 4 is REV, how much of this track feeds the reverb; take it up around 60–80 and hold a chord. KNOB 3 is DLY, the delay: a quieter echo of everything you play, repeating in time. A little of both and the room gets much bigger. Tap HOME to come back to TRACKS.

GarageBand translation

Reverb and delay here are send effects, exactly like GarageBand's Master Echo and Reverb sliders on a track: the dial sets how much of this track is poured into a shared effect, and every track has its own pour.

Let the sound hold itself

A pad only becomes an atmosphere when it keeps sounding while your hands do other things. SLOOP has a lovely feature for exactly this, keyboard hold:

  1. Tap ARP until the page titled ARP 2 shows (or tap once and walk there with SELECT).

  2. Turn KNOB 3 to set HOLD ON. (The arpeggiator itself stays OFF; with the arp off, HOLD simply sustains whatever you play.)

  3. Tap HOME, hold a chord, and let go. It keeps sounding. Play a new chord with no keys down and it replaces the old one; add notes while keys are still down and they join in.

To silence it: set HOLD back to OFF on ARP 2, or load another sound. Up to eight notes can sound across the three synth tracks, so a four‑note chord leaves room to spare.

Three‑note chords suit them beautifully.

you should now be hearing

A held chord washing through reverb and delay, sustaining on its own while your hands are off the keys. That is an atmosphere, and it took four knobs.

try this

Audition every pad in the shortlist and crown two favourites. On the SAVE pages' PRESETS page (tap ENV then SAVE; SELECT to PRESETS if needed), turn KNOB 4 one click right: FAV ADDED. Favourites jump to the front of the PRESETS list, so your sounds are always two clicks away.

made a mess?

A note will not stop sounding: that is HOLD doing its job; set HOLD OFF on ARP 2, or load any other sound. The sound itself got strange after knob exploring: just re‑select it with PRESETS (one click away and back), which reloads the preset fresh. Nothing in this lesson is recorded, so there is nothing to undo.

L2Lesson two · Atmosphere

Shaping a sound

Three ideas, envelope, LFO and filter, are most of what "synthesis" means. Each is one page of four knobs, and each makes immediate sense on a pad.

starting from here
  • Track 1 selected, a pad loaded (WARM PAD is perfect), some reverb on it, as in Lesson 1.
  • HOLD can be on or off; off is less confusing while you experiment.

The envelope: how a note fades in and out

Tap ENV (the page titled ENV; tap again if ENV DEST shows instead). Four dials: ATK (attack), DEC (decay), SUS (sustain) and REL (release). Together they draw the shape of every note's loudness:

  1. Raise ATK well up and play a chord: it now blooms instead of starting. This single move turns almost any sound into a pad.

  2. Raise REL too: when you let go, the chord exhales slowly rather than stopping. Long attack, long release, and you can play lazily; the sound smooths everything over.

The filter: one knob of brightness

Hold FX and turn KNOB 4: this is the track's filter on one dial. Left of centre it is a low‑pass, closing the sound down to something dark and distant; right of centre a high‑pass, thinning it to a whisper; centre is off. To sweep it while a chord sounds, let the chord hold itself first (KBD HOLD from Lesson 1), since one hand is on FX and the other on the knob; then sweep slowly and you will hear why filters are the most loved control in electronic music. It stays where you leave it, even when you change sounds.

GarageBand translation

A low‑pass filter is the "cutoff" dial on GarageBand's synth Smart Controls, or an EQ with the treble pulled right down: the tone control of the synth world. Cutoff just means the point where brightness stops.

The LFO: movement you do not have to play

An LFO (low‑frequency oscillator) is an invisible hand that slowly turns a knob for you, round and round, forever. Point it at the filter and the sound breathes; at the pitch and it shimmers; at the volume and it swells and recedes like a tide.

  1. Tap LFO until the page titled LFO shows. Set RATE slow (well left), WAVE to SIN (a smooth sine), and give FADE a little so the movement eases in.

  2. Tap LFO until LFO DEST shows. Turn FLT up a modest amount. Hold a chord: the brightness now slowly rises and falls on its own.

  3. Tap LFO until the page titled LFO 2 shows, and set SYNC to 2BAR or 4BAR: the cycle now locks to the tempo, one slow breath every two or four bars, perfect for loops later. (RATE is ignored while SYNC is on.)

you should now be hearing

A chord that blooms in, breathes in brightness by itself, and exhales when released. You have just done sound design.

try this

On LFO DEST, set FLT back near zero and raise AMP instead: now the volume breathes, a slow tremolo. With SYNC at 4BAR and POL set to UNI on LFO 2 (so it only dips, never boosts), a held drone turns into a tide.

A word you now own

Modulation is the general word for "something automatically moving a control": the LFO modulates the filter. That is the entire mystery of the term.

made a mess?

Too much wobble, lost track of what you changed? Re‑select the sound with PRESETS to reload it clean. Or start the sound from scratch: on the SAVE pages' TOOLS page, INIT (KNOB 2, the two‑click GO) resets the track's sound to its engine's defaults. Your other tracks are untouched either way.

L3Lesson three · Atmosphere

Record your first loop

SLOOP's recorder is built around a beautiful idea: play first, and let the loop form around what you played. If you can count four beats, you already have the only skill this needs.

starting from here
  • An empty loop (the clean‑slate NEW from Lesson 1's start box if anything is lying around), so the free take is available.
  • Track 1 selected with a pad you like; HOLD off for now (ARP 2 page), so notes end when your fingers lift.

The free take: the loop follows you

  1. Press REC while stopped. The screen says rec ready · play freely; the REC light blinks. Nothing is recording yet.

  2. Play. At your own pace, no click, no countdown: a chord, a change, another. From your first note SLOOP is listening, showing the seconds and the loop it would make right now (2 bars · 92 bpm).

  3. Count yourself round, and press REC on the "1" after your last bar. That press is the only timing that matters: it tells SLOOP where the loop ends. The loop closes, your playing snaps gently into it, the tempo is set from how you played, and it starts looping immediately.

SLOOP chooses 1, 2 or 4 bars, whichever best fits what you played, and all four tracks adopt that length. Change your mind mid‑take? PLAY drops the take; a take also closes by itself after 24 seconds.

GarageBand translation

This is cycle recording without drawing the cycle region first: you perform, then mark the end, and the region is cut to fit. "Snapping to the grid" is quantise, the same tidy‑up GarageBand applies to MIDI regions.

Prefer a count‑in? There is one

Once a loop exists (or any time you would rather set the tempo first), recording works the familiar way: set the tempo with SELECT on TRACKS, press REC while stopped, and on the armed screen use the four dials: mode (on an empty loop: free or tempo), length (1, 2 or 4 bars), start (note: your first note begins the loop · count: press PLAY for one bar of clicks, 4‑3‑2‑1 on screen, then recording begins), and quant (on: notes land on the grid · off: your exact timing is kept). These are device settings; they stay as you leave them.

Layering: the overdub

  1. With the loop playing, press REC: recording starts immediately on the selected track, on top of what is there. Play a few more notes across a pass or two, then press REC again to stop recording; the loop plays on.

  2. Held notes record as held notes, chords as chords, and everything you layer joins the same loop. This stack‑another‑pass habit is the heart of SLOOP.

The quiet metronome

The PLAY light flashes on every beat, always: a silent conductor. If you want an audible click, it lives at GLO → GLOBAL → CLICK (OFF, REC = only while recording, ON). The click is never recorded.

you should now be hearing

A one‑, two‑ or four‑bar pad progression looping by itself, at a tempo you set just by playing, with a second pass layered over it.

try this

Undo the pass you just made (EDIT+OCT−), then record the same chords again with QUANT off and compare: the loop keeps your human timing. Then open the ENV page while the loop plays and slowly raise ATK: you can redesign the sound of notes that are already recorded, because SLOOP stores the notes, not the audio.

made a mess?

Three sizes of eraser, gentlest first. Undo the last pass: EDIT+OCT− (redo: EDIT+OCT+; one level, covering the last recording pass or edit). Clear just this track: hold REC about two seconds; a ring fills on screen, and letting go early cancels. Undo can bring a cleared track back. Start the whole loop over: the NEW tool from Lesson 1's start box. And remember autosave: stop playback, hands off for a moment, and the current state is kept through power‑off.

L4Lesson four · Atmosphere

Weather: textures and dust

The secret glue of ambient and lo‑fi records is a quiet bed of noise underneath everything: rain, tape hiss, vinyl crackle. SLOOP generates these for you, without using up a track.

starting from here
  • Any loop playing (Lesson 3's, ideally), or even just a held pad. Any track can be selected; this lesson's settings are global.

  1. Tap GLO repeatedly until the TEXTURE page shows.

  2. Turn SRC to RAIN. Bring LEVEL up until it sits just under the music. Try the other weathers: VINYL (hiss, rumble and crackle), TAPE, WIND, RADIO, ROOM.

  3. TONE darkens (left) or thins (right) the bed; MOVE makes it breathe, swelling and receding. On the next page, TEXTURE 2, RATE sets how long each breath takes, 1 to 16 bars of your tempo.

  4. One more colour: hold FX and turn KNOB 2, DUST. It runs the whole mix through an old sampler and a worn record: softly driven, fewer bits, a little hiss and crackle while the music plays. A small amount warms everything.

you should now be hearing

Your loop sitting inside weather: rain breathing beneath it, a little dust on top. It instantly sounds like a finished record.

try this

Set TEXTURE 2's WHEN to PLAY so the weather fades in and out with the transport: press PLAY to stop and the rain politely stops with the band.

made a mess?

Everything here is reversible by turning it off: SRC to OFF silences the bed entirely (and costs nothing while off), DUST back to 0 cleans the mix. Nothing in this lesson records into your loop.

L5Lesson five · Atmosphere

Playing in key

Here is the deal SLOOP offers: tell it one note, and it will quietly make wrong notes impossible. You get to sound like you know music theory years before you do.

starting from here
  • Track 2 selected (green), which starts life as a RHODES electric piano: lovely for chords.
  • A loop can be playing or not; this works live either way.

Three words, then the keys do the theory

Key: the home note a piece of music gravitates to. Scale: the family of notes that belong with that home. Chord: three or four of those notes sounding together. That is all the theory this book needs.

  1. Choose the key. Hold SEL and press any key, say the one labelled D on page 8's map. The screen says KEY D, and all three synth tracks now treat D as home.

  2. Choose the mood. Still holding SEL, turn KNOB 2, SCALE. MAJ is bright, MIN is melancholy; for atmospheres also try DOR (minor with a glow), LYD (floating), MPEN (minor pentatonic: five notes, nothing can clash).

  3. Make wrong notes impossible. SEL+KNOB 3 is KEYS. On SNAP, anything you play is nudged into the scale. On WHITE, the white keys simply walk the scale upward from key 5 (C4), and the black keys fall silent.

  4. One finger, whole chords. SEL+KNOB 1 is CHORD: set it to TRIAD, the plain three‑note chord. Now every white key plays a full, correct chord of your key: key 5 is the home chord, and each key up plays the next one along. That is the whole mechanism; richer voicings wait on the next page.

GarageBand translation

This is Smart Instrument chord strips, generalised: the instrument constrains you to the key so every choice is a good one. Unlike chord strips, you pick the key, scale and voicing yourself.

The main exercise: a progression in one take

  1. With CHORD on TRIAD and your Rhodes sounding, practise a slow four‑chord journey on single white keys until it feels natural: for instance key 5, key 10, key 8, key 9, one chord per bar, four bars in all (a free take closes at 1, 2 or 4 bars, so four fits it exactly; chords that each last two bars come later, in Lesson 8's slow sequencer).

  2. Record it in one pass. If you are on an empty loop, free‑take it exactly as in Lesson 3: REC, play the four bars, REC on the "1" after the fourth. If a shorter loop from Lesson 3 is already playing, give this track four bars first: stop, press REC to arm, set the armed screen's LENGTH dial to 4 bars, then play; your shorter pad simply repeats underneath, in time. Whatever you hear is exactly what records.

  3. One learned flourish: record the take again (undo first: EDIT+OCT−), and this time hold the black key G# while you play one chord of the cycle. G# adds the 7th, so that one chord records richer, every time around. One black key, one chord, is plenty.

you should now be hearing

A chord progression that sounds deliberate and harmonically "right", looping under your pad, played with one finger per chord, with a single 7th glowing in it.

Optional exploration: CHORD+, the black keys bend the chord

When the basics feel comfortable, the other black keys open up: each one edits the chord under your finger while held (press the black key with the white one, or while the chord still sounds):

Both octaves of black keys work, you can hold several at once, and the FM‑1 also offers 7TH, 9TH, SUS4 and POWER as standing chord modes on SEL+KNOB 1, once you know which colour you want everywhere.

try this

Tap SEL until the page titled SEL 2 shows. STRUM rolls each chord's notes like a strummed guitar (right: low to high); VLEAD ON is voice leading: each chord voiced as close as possible to the last, so progressions glide instead of jumping. VLEAD on a pad is pure atmosphere.

made a mess?

Black keys gone silent on a synth track? That is chord or scale mode working as designed: set CHORD to OFF and KEYS to OFF (both on the SEL hold) and the keyboard is fully chromatic again. A recorded pass you regret: EDIT+OCT− undoes it. Recorded chords keep the notes they were played as; if a loop clashes after a key change, undo that take and record it again.

L6Lesson six · Rhythm

Drums, gently

Track 4 is a sixteen‑sound drum machine played on the white keys. We will start with kits that whisper rather than bang, and record a beat the same forgiving way you recorded your pads.

starting from here
  • Ideally, your pad loop from Lessons 3 or 5 is on the device (autosave will have kept it). An empty loop also works; you will just free‑take the drums first instead.
  • Turn ALGORITHM to track 4. The screen turns orange.

The sixteen sounds, one per white key

The two keys you will lean on are shaded: key 1 (kick) and key 5 (hat). A black key plays the same sound as the white key to its left, so two fingers can share one drum for fast patterns. The closed and pedal hats automatically cut the open hat short, as on a real kit.

  1. Choose a gentle kit. Turn PRESETS: on track 4 it browses the 37 drum kits. For this music, audition AMBIENT, LO‑FI, JAZZ, BOOMBAP and DUST (a hazy treatment of the sampled acoustic kit). Changing kit never changes a recorded pattern, so you can swap kits under a beat forever.

  2. Learn the dynamics trick. Hold OCT− while you hit a drum: a ghost note, quiet and dark. Hold OCT+: a hard hit. The FM‑1's keys cannot feel how hard you press, so these two buttons are your touch sensitivity, and they record exactly as played.

A pattern to copy, and a take you can count

If a blank bar feels like a dare, borrow this one. Sixteen steps make one bar (at the default step size of 1/16), counted 1 2 3 4 across it; the pattern below is the gentle backbone of half the lo‑fi records ever made.

Solid orange = a normal hit; pale orange = a ghost (hold OCT− as you play it). Kick on the "1" and once more on the "and" after beat 3, snare on beats 2 and 4, straight hats with every second one kept quiet. Play it slowly on the keys first; it needs only three fingers.

A counted four‑bar take, start to finish

This is the count‑in workflow from Lesson 3, written out once in full on an empty loop, so there is no guessing:

  1. On TRACKS, check the tempo (a kindly 75–85 is lovely; keep whatever a free take already set if you have one). Make sure track 4 is selected.

  2. Press REC while stopped, then set the dials on the armed screen: LENGTH 4 bars · START count · QUANT on (on a completely empty loop a fourth dial, MODE, appears first: set it to tempo; once any track holds notes that dial is gone and the set tempo is simply used).

  3. Press PLAY. One bar of clicks counts you in, 4‑3‑2‑1 on the screen, then recording begins.

  4. Play the pattern above, round and round, counting 1 2 3 4 with the click, for four bars (four counts of four). Perfection not required: quantise tidies the timing.

  5. Press REC. Recording stops and your four‑bar beat plays on, looping. (Changed your mind during the count‑in? REC cancels it.)

Why four bars and not one

A longer loop leaves room for the pattern to vary: drop the kick syncopation in bars 1 and 2, let it in for bars 3 and 4, and the beat breathes without any extra machinery.

Record a beat under your loop

  1. Press PLAY so your pad loop runs (empty device? use the counted take on the previous page, or free‑take the drums as in Lesson 3).

  2. Press REC while it plays: track 4 starts recording immediately. Lay down just a kick pattern on key 1 on the first pass, nothing else. Press REC to stop recording and listen to it loop.

  3. REC again and add hats on key 5, holding OCT− for most of them so they sit back as ghosts. Another pass for a soft snare or rim on the backbeat (keys 3 or 8, on counts 2 and 4). Build the beat in quiet layers, one sound per pass.

  4. A roll without the finger work: hold ARP and hold the hat key: it repeats in perfect time at the rate on KNOB 1 (1/16 to start). Recorded while a take runs, rolls are written into the pattern as repeats.

  5. Swing it. On TRACKS, KNOB 1 is global swing: push it to 55–62 and straight hats start to saunter. 0 is rigid; 100 is the strongest classic MPC lope.

Space for the drums too

That REVERB dial on the kit page is how far away the drums stand. For this style of music, a generous send and a quiet level beats a loud dry kit every time.

you should now be hearing

A soft, swung beat breathing under your pad loop: ghost hats, a patient kick, a snare that taps rather than cracks.

try this

Overdub a shaker (key 14) played only as ghosts, every eighth note. Then swap kits with PRESETS while the beat plays and hear the same pattern wearing different clothes.

made a mess?

To erase one drum from the beat, hold EDIT and hold that drum's key while the loop plays: it is wiped from every step the playhead passes while you hold it (hold it for one full bar and that bar's hats are gone). Do the same while stopped and the sound is removed from the whole pattern at once. The broader erasers still apply: EDIT+OCT− undoes the last pass, and holding REC ~2 s clears track 4 entirely.

L7Lesson seven · Rhythm

Bass, melody and the mix

A pad, a beat, and now the two voices that complete it: something low holding the floor, something light carrying a tune. Then you will balance all four layers like a tiny mixing desk.

starting from here
  • Pad on track 1, chords or Rhodes on track 2, beat on track 4, all looping (Lessons 3–6). Fewer layers is fine too.
  • Key and scale set from Lesson 5, so the bass and melody land in tune automatically.

The low end

  1. Turn ALGORITHM to track 3 (yellow) and browse PRESETS to the basses. SUB BASS is deep and pure; 808 BOOM hums and slides between held notes; DEEP BASS and PLUCK BASS are rounder and shorter. The 808‑family and sub basses sound two octaves below the printed keys, so key 5 is already properly low.

  2. KEYS is a per‑track setting, so give this track the safety net you gave track 2: hold SEL and turn KNOB 3 to SNAP. Then record a bass line of long single notes, mostly the home key, moving once or twice a bar. Most basses are MONO, one note at a time, which is exactly what a bass line wants.

Small speakers and big bass

The FM‑1's built‑in speaker cannot reproduce very low notes; headphones or a proper speaker reveal what the sub is actually doing.

A melody that floats, and the three‑track reality

One honest constraint first: a synth track holds one sound at a time, and that sound plays everything recorded on the track. Turn PRESETS on track 2 and your recorded Rhodes chords become kalimba chords: the sequencer stores notes, the track supplies the voice. GarageBand gives unlimited tracks; the FM‑1 gives three melodic voices plus drums, and choosing which three deserve the song is half the craft. A melody has three homes:

  • Share track 2's voice (the simple path): overdub the melody onto track 2 an octave up, played in the same Rhodes. One instrument, two roles, and it always blends. One limit to know: a step holds at most four notes, so three‑note TRIAD chords leave exactly one voice spare per step; place melody notes mostly between the chord hits, and if one lands on a full step the oldest held note politely gives way.
  • Trade the chords away: clear or thin track 2, load KALIMBA, MUSIC BOX or MARIMBA, and let the pad carry the harmony alone. Minimal suits this music.
  • Save it for a section: in Lesson 10, each saved section stores its own sounds per track, so section A can be Rhodes chords and section B the kalimba melody.

  1. Take the simple path now: stay on track 2, set CHORD to OFF for single notes (hold SEL, KNOB 1), keep KEYS on SNAP, tap OCT+ up an octave, and press REC while the loop plays.

  2. Record sparse phrases: three or four notes, then silence. In this genre the gaps are the melody. (Set CHORD back afterwards if you still want the chord keys live.)

Mixing: the GLO layer is your desk

Hold GLO and the FM‑1 becomes a four‑channel mixer for as long as you hold it:

While holding GLODoes
keys 1–4mute / unmute tracks 1–4 (a quick fade; the pattern keeps running in time underneath)
keys 5–8solo tracks 1–4 (several solos add together)
KNOB 1–4the levels of tracks 1–4
key 16tap tempo (two or more taps)

Balance the four layers so the pad is a bed, the bass is felt, the drums whisper and the melody floats on top. Two finer controls live on TRACKS: KNOB 2 is the selected track's level and KNOB 4 its pan (left–right position); nudging the melody slightly off‑centre adds air.

you should now be hearing

A complete four‑layer piece: pad, chords or melody, bass, beat, balanced so no layer shouts. This is a track. Yours.

GarageBand translation

GLO is the mixer column of every GarageBand track, compressed into one held button: M, S, volume fader and pan, for all four tracks at once.

try this

Perform the mix: hold GLO and pull tracks in and out with the mute keys, starting from just pad and texture, adding bass, then drums, then melody. You have just discovered the arrangement moves in Lesson 10. (Need both hands? Hold GLO and tap HOME to lock the mixer open; any other button releases it.)

made a mess?

Nothing here destroys anything: a muted track is still there (unmute it with the same key, or on TRACKS turn its level knob, which unmutes as it moves), solos stack until un‑soloed, and levels are just levels. If a recorded bass or melody pass misfires, the usual EDIT+OCT− undoes it.

L8Lesson eight · Rhythm

The step sequencer, essentials only

Everything so far was played live. The step sequencer is the other door into the same patterns: a grid where you place and remove hits by hand, perfect for precision and for slow experiments.

starting from here
  • Your loop from the last lessons, playing or stopped. Select track 4 for the first half of this lesson.

The DRUMS grid: see the whole beat

  1. On TRACKS with track 4 selected, tap SEQ: the DRUMS grid, sixteen sounds down, sixteen steps across, your recorded beat drawn as marks (louder hits brighter).

  2. KNOB 1 picks the sound row, KNOB 2 the step, KNOB 3 turned right places a hit there (left clears it), KNOB 4 sets that hit's level, ghost to hard.

  3. Faster with the keys: the 16 white keys are the 16 steps of the sound currently picked, on the page shown; press one to set a step, press it again to clear it. The black keys silently switch which sound you are editing (some black keys cover a pair, press again for the pair's other sound; others pick a single sound).

  4. Longer beats have pages. A pattern can run to 64 steps and the screen shows 16 at a time: OCT− / OCT+ turn the page (1–16, 17–32, 33–48, 49–64, up to the track's length). The counted four‑bar take from Lesson 6 spans four pages, and the keys always edit the page on screen.

The SEQ layer: the same grid, anywhere

On any track, hold SEQ: the white keys become the pattern's 16 steps (lit = plays). Tap an empty step to set it (drums: with the sound shown; synths: with the last note or chord you played), tap a lit one to clear it. Longer patterns page here too: with SEQ held, OCT− / OCT+ turn the page of steps (on a synth track, the first four black keys as well). While holding SEQ, KNOB 2 is DIV and KNOB 4 is LENGTH; those two are the subject of the next page.

GarageBand translation

This is GarageBand's Beat Sequencer: the same rows‑of‑cells idea, driven by knobs and keys instead of taps on glass.

Length, division, and the slow‑music trick

Each track's pattern has its own LENGTH (1 to 64 steps) and its own DIV, how much musical time one step lasts: from 1/32 of a bar up to one or even two whole bars per step. Both live on the SEQ hold (KNOB 4 and KNOB 2). One honest mechanic first: changing DIV never moves notes between steps; every step simply lasts longer or shorter, so flipping a recorded 1/16 pattern to 1BAR just stretches the whole take sixteen‑fold. Slow music is best built slow:

Build a slow chord bed from a clean start

This replaces the pad's pattern, so park the loop somewhere safe first: hold SAVE — among keys 5–8, a dark key is an empty section, and pressing a dark one saves the whole loop there without touching anything else (lit keys already hold music, so leave those alone; Lesson 10 explains sections). If all four are lit, or you would rather not touch sections yet, postpone this exercise until after Lesson 10; nothing else depends on it.

  1. Select the pad, track 1, and clear its pattern: hold REC about two seconds until the ring fills. (EDIT+OCT− right now brings it straight back.)

  2. Hold SEQ: turn KNOB 4 (LENGTH) to 4 and KNOB 2 (DIV) to 1BAR. Four steps, each lasting a whole bar.

  3. Give this track chords too: CHORD is per‑track (Lesson 5 set it on track 2 only), so hold SEL and turn KNOB 1 to TRIAD here; key and scale are shared by all synth tracks and already right.

  4. Write a known chord onto each step (an empty step takes the chord you played last): play key 5 (the home chord, I), then SEQ+key 1 to put it on step 1; play key 10 (VI), then SEQ+key 2 for step 2; play key 8 (IV), then SEQ+key 3 for step 3; play key 9 (V), then SEQ+key 4 for step 4.

  5. PLAY. The pad changes chord once a bar (how much of each bar it rings is the PATTERN page's GATE; your pad's long release does the rest) while the drums tick in 1/16 underneath. For glacial: DIV 2BAR, one chord every two bars.

Polymeter, free of charge

Length belongs to a whole track (all sixteen drum sounds share track 4's one pattern and length), so the trick lives between tracks: set this chord bed's LENGTH to 3 and a three‑chord round turns against the four‑bar drums, realigning every twelve bars. Set it back to 4 when done; the fourth chord returns, since shortening hides far steps rather than erasing them.

you should now be hearing

A beat you can see and edit cell by cell, and a chord bed you placed by hand that changes on a one‑bar clock over drums ticking in 1/16: two timescales in one loop.

try this

On the grid, demote every hat except the ones on the beat to ghost with KNOB 4, and push one unexpected hat to hard. Tiny level moves like this are where grooves actually live.

made a mess?

A step you just set: tap its key again (SEQ held, or on the grid) and it is gone; EDIT+OCT− undoes the last change (one level, so after further edits the road back to the old pad pass is to record it again). A silent synth pattern? Check DIV: at 1BAR it crawls, and may not have reached its next step yet.

L9Lesson nine · Performing

Live hands: punch‑in effects and fills

Hold FX and the whole keyboard becomes 27 momentary effects on the entire mix: each one lasts exactly as long as your finger does, lands in time, and lets go cleanly. This is how a loop becomes a performance.

starting from here
  • Your four‑layer loop playing. Nothing else matters; punch‑ins work on whatever is sounding, and none of them is ever recorded into your loop.

Six to learn first (an ambient hand's picks)

Hold FX, then holdEffect
A#4 (black)FREEZE: the mix is poured into the reverb and held there, a sustained cloud, for as long as you hold the key; let go and it dies away slowly
key 9low‑pass sweep: the music sinks underwater and returns
key 10high‑pass sweep: everything thins to a distant shimmer
key 7tape stop: the record winds down to silence
key 8half speed: everything an octave down, at half pace
F#5 (black)FADE OUT: the mix fades to nothing over two beats, the politest ending in music

The other 21 are drawn on screen while FX is held (loops of various lengths, stutter, reverse, bit crush, kill‑EQ keys and more); explore freely, nothing sticks. Since 2.8 you can soften how effects enter and leave: hold FX and turn SELECT, 0 (instant) to 15 (about a third of a second).

The knobs while FX is held

KNOB 1 master FILTER (the classic DJ sweep) · KNOB 2 DUST · KNOB 3 DUCK (every kick gently pumps the synths down and back, the "sidechain" sound) · KNOB 4 the selected track's own filter.

Two throws: hold FX+OCT− and every track's reverb send goes full while held; FX+OCT+ does the same for the delay. Your saved mix is never changed.

Both hands free

Hold FX, tap HOME: the FX layer locks open (the button blinks). One hand rides FREEZE, the other the filter. Any other button releases the lock.

Fills: a one‑finger "something is about to happen"

  1. Hold GLO and hold key 9: a fill runs for as long as you hold it. Hold GLO and tap key 10 instead and the whole next bar plays as a fill, then everything returns to normal (tap again before the bar to cancel).

  2. Out of the box a fill changes nothing audible, because no step in your patterns is marked for it yet: fills act only on steps you label fill‑only (they play during fills) or no‑fill (they drop out). The full system is on page 31; the sixty‑second taste below makes one fill real right now.

Make one fill audible, in four moves

1. On the drum track, hold SEQ and press the black key A#4: the sound under edit becomes CRASH, silently. Still holding SEQ, turn the page with OCT− / OCT+ until the title reads page 1 (steps 1–16).

2. Still holding SEQ, glance at key 16's tile: in the Lesson 6 pattern it is empty (unlit); if you have reshaped your beat since, pick any unlit step at a bar's end instead. Tap it: a crash lands there. Empty on purpose: a fill condition belongs to the whole step, every sound in it, so marking a busy step (step 1, say, with its kick and hat) would drag those hits into fill‑only too.

3. Still holding SEQ, now hold that step key and tap OCT+ once: a small F marks the tile. The crash now plays only during fills.

4. Let everything go, then hold GLO and hold key 9 through one full round of the loop: the moment the playhead crosses that step with the fill held, the crash rings; let go and it vanishes again. (The four‑bar pattern only passes step 16 once a round, so for a crash that greets every fill bar, repeat moves 2–3 on the last step of pages 2, 3 and 4 as well, OCT+ turning the pages while SEQ is held. Remove any of them later: SEQ held, tap the step.)

you should now be hearing

Your loop as a living thing: frozen into reverb clouds, swept underwater, wound down and restarted, all with single fingers and all without recording a thing.

try this

Practise one complete transition, one move at a time. The layers are exclusive: whichever function button is held owns the keys (and FX outranks the others), so chain the moves rather than stacking them. Hold FX with FREEZE for two bars → let both go → straight onto GLO, unmute the drums → still on GLO, tap key 10 for a fill bar → hands off. That is a chorus entrance, performed.

made a mess?

The punch‑ins themselves make no mess: effects and throws end the moment you release them, and are never recorded. Two things from this page do persist. The knobs: if the mix sounds odd afterwards, hold FX and set KNOB 1 (FILTER) back to centre, KNOB 2/KNOB 3 (DUST, DUCK) back toward 0, and check the track filter, KNOB 4, is centred. And the fill crash: that was a real pattern edit, so the step and its F condition stay in your pattern, through autosave and into any section you save, until you remove them — hold SEQ, pick CRASH with A#4, and tap its step.

GarageBand translation

Punch‑ins are GarageBand's Remix FX (the DJ pad with filter, gater, downsampler and vinyl stop), mapped to real keys, always in tempo, and strictly momentary.

L10Lesson ten · Keeping it

Sections, songs, and saving your music

So far you have one loop. A piece of music is several: an intro, a verse, a lift, an ending. SLOOP calls these sections A to D, and you move between them live, on the bar, with one hand.

starting from here
  • The loop you have built across this book, playing or stopped. Everything here lives on the SAVE hold.

  1. Save the loop as section A: hold SAVE and press key 5. (Saving over a section that already holds something asks you to press the same key again within 3 seconds.)

  2. Make a variation. Change the loop: mute the drums and thin the pad (a quieter "verse"), or add a second melody pass. Save it into B: SAVE+key 6. A lifted version into C (key 7), an ending into D (key 8).

  3. Play them live: hold SAVE and press keys 1–4 for A–D. While music plays, the new section begins on the next bar, every track from its first step, always in time. (Stopped, it loads immediately.)

  4. Chain without hands: keep SAVE held and tap several section keys in a row, A B B C, up to eight, repeats welcome. Let go: they play in turn, round and round.

  5. Record the arrangement: SAVE+key 14 starts SONG REC: from the next bar, every section you play, and for how long, is written down as the song. The same combination (or stopping) ends it. SAVE+key 13 then flips between loop and song mode; in song mode, PLAY performs the whole written song and stops at the end.

GarageBand translation

Sections are GarageBand's song sections in Live Loops clothing: each one is a full snapshot of all four tracks, and SONG REC is "record your Live Loops performance into an arrangement".

What is actually saved where

A section is a complete snapshot: all four tracks (sounds, patterns, kit, filters) plus the loop's tempo, swing, key and texture. Track mutes, levels and pans save with it too (confirmed in the firmware source), so a muted‑drums "verse" stores faithfully; the per‑sound drum mutes from EDIT's MUTE mode are the one performance tool that does not. Your working loop is separate from the four sections: loading a section copies it into the working loop, saving copies the working loop into a section. The FM‑1 holds one song at a time: four sections and the order they play in.

Keeping more than one piece of music

A to D are four sections of one song, not four songs. To keep several pieces, save one web‑editor backup file per song (Chapter 2) and restore whichever you want to play; the editor's Song & sections page can also export a single song as its own small file. This is the one habit that protects weeks of work, so make it a ritual: finish a piece, save a backup, name the file after the song.

you should now be hearing

A piece with a shape: sections handed over on the bar, a written arrangement that plays itself start to finish, and a backup file on your Mac with its name on it.

try this

Open the SONG screen (SAVE+key 16), stop playback if it is running (the song's settings edit only while stopped), and edit the written song by hand: KNOB 1 walks the steps, KNOB 2 sets each step's section, KNOB 3 its length in bars. Stretch your ending section to eight bars.

The web editor in ten minutes

Everything on the FM‑1, mirrored live in a browser tab: bigger screen, mouse‑draggable knobs, and the doors to backups, your own samples and much more. You met it in Chapter 2; here is the guided lap.

  1. Open isod89.github.io/sloop-fm1/webapp/editor in Chrome or Edge, FM‑1 on USB, press Connect. The editor follows the device live: turn a knob on the FM‑1 and the screen value moves, and vice versa. Every value is a tile you drag up or down like a knob; double‑click resets it.

  2. Walk the sidebar. Sound: every parameter of the selected track as cards, one per button. Sequencer: the pattern as a grid or piano roll, clickable. Library: a sound library kept in your browser, searchable and taggable, with drag and drop to the device's 32 user slots. Song & sections: load and save A–D, export a song as a file, and the Backup button from Chapter 2. LIVE: the whole instrument laid out for performing from the computer, phone or tablet.

  3. Record your music into the Mac: on USB the FM‑1 is also an audio input called Felucca: everything the headphones hear, dust, texture and punch‑ins included, offered to any recording app. The box below walks it through for GarageBand.

Recording into GarageBand, step by step

1. FM‑1 on USB; on the device, hold HOME → AUDIO → set USB AUDIO to FULL, so the Mac receives a fixed, healthy level whatever the MASTER knob does.

2. In GarageBand, open Settings → Audio/MIDI and choose Felucca as the Input Device (your speakers stay the output).

3. Create an Audio track (the microphone icon), input channels 1 + 2 (stereo), and leave software monitoring off so you listen on the FM‑1's own output.

4. Arm the track, press GarageBand's record button, then press PLAY on the FM‑1 (or play live). Stop both when done: your piece is now audio on the Mac, ready to trim, share, or build on.

GarageBand's exact menu names shift between versions; the constant parts are the input device named Felucca, a stereo audio track, and USB AUDIO = FULL on the FM‑1. One cable carries MIDI, the editor and this audio at once.

Three small delights

The editor installs as an offline app (the Install the app link under Connect) so it works without internet. It speaks to an audience too: the LIVE page has a help "?" on every card. And you can try the whole editor with no FM‑1 attached by adding ?mock=1 to its address, a pleasant way to explore from the couch.

made a mess?

The editor cannot find the FM‑1: Chrome or Edge only, a data cable, no hub, allow MIDI, and close any other app or tab using MIDI, then reload. The audio input does not appear on the Mac: replug the FM‑1, and in your recording app rescan audio devices. Flash writes (presets, backups) politely wait until the song is stopped; the editor will ask.

Glossary

Plain‑words glossary

Every musical and synthesis term this book used, each in one breath.

Bar · beat · BPM
Four beats make a bar in this book's music; BPM is beats per minute, the tempo.
Chord · key · scale
A chord is a few notes sounding together; the key is the home note music gravitates to; the scale is the family of notes that belong with it.
Cutoff
The point where a filter stops letting brightness through.
Delay
A repeating echo, in time with the tempo.
Division (DIV)
How much musical time one sequencer step lasts, from 1/32 of a bar to two whole bars.
Engine · preset
An engine is a way of making sound (SLOOP has twelve); a preset is a finished sound built on one.
Envelope (ATK/DEC/SUS/REL)
The shape of one note's loudness: fade in, settle, held level, fade out.
Filter (low‑pass / high‑pass)
A tone control: low‑pass darkens by removing highs; high‑pass thins by removing lows.
Free take
SLOOP's "play first" recording: perform freely, press REC on the "1" after your last bar, and the loop forms around what you played.
Ghost note
A deliberately quiet hit that gives a groove its texture.
LFO
A slow, invisible hand turning a control round and round: movement you do not have to play.
Modulation
Anything automatically moving a control, such as an LFO moving a filter.
Mono · poly · voice
A voice is one note sounding; mono sounds play one voice at a time, poly several.
Overdub
Recording another pass on top of what is already in the loop.
Punch‑in effect
A momentary effect on the whole mix that lasts exactly as long as the key is held and is never recorded.
Quantise (QUANT)
Nudging recorded notes onto the timing grid; off, your human timing is kept.
Ratchet
A hit repeating two, three or four times inside its own step.
Reverb · send
Reverb is the sound of a space; a send is how much of a track you pour into a shared effect.
Section
A saved snapshot of the whole loop (all four tracks and its settings); SLOOP holds four, A to D, and a song is sections in order.
Sidechain (DUCK)
The kick momentarily pushing everything else down, giving electronic music its pumping breath.
Step sequencer
A grid of on/off cells that plays a pattern; what you record live and what you place by hand are the same pattern.
Swing
Delaying every second step a little, so straight time starts to saunter.
Texture
SLOOP's generated bed of rain, wind, vinyl or tape under the whole mix.
Troubleshooting & links

When something seems wrong

SymptomCure
The installer or editor cannot find the FM‑1Chrome or Edge, a data cable, no hub, allow MIDI; close every other app or tab using MIDI, then reload
The black keys are silent on a synth trackChord or scale mode is on (by design): set CHORD OFF and KEYS OFF on the SEL hold
Recorded notes moved from where I played themQuantise at work: record with QUANT off (armed screen, KNOB 4) to keep your timing
Notes fade out when many sound at onceThe processor is at its limit; SLOOP fades one voice at a time rather than glitching. Fewer held notes, or a lighter sound, helps
The USB recording is quietHold HOME → AUDIO → USB AUDIO = FULL, or turn MASTER up
The USB audio input does not appearReplug the FM‑1; in your recording app, rescan audio devices
STOP THE SONG FIRST / STOP FIRSTThat change waits until playback is stopped; press PLAY and try again
EMPTY SECTION: RECThat section has nothing saved in it yet: SAVE+keys 5–8 first
